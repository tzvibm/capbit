The vocabulary fix

The thread was confusing because engine words leaked into the interface. A user does not think "which engagement is armed" — they think "I bought ten answers from Maya, six are left." Every internal concept now has a plain-language surface word, and the UI only ever uses the right column.

Why "answer" is the right word

It maps to how people already think about paid advice: you ask a question, you get an answer, and that's the thing of value you bought. It makes the free/paid split self-explanatory — a clarifying question from the coach is obviously not an answer — and it's countable, so "6 answers left" needs no explanation. Where a coach sells a different unit, the unit label carries through verbatim: Sam's photo review reads "3 of 4 photos left", a live call reads "18:42 left".

Client · items sheet

What changed and why

1"Using · Convo pack — 6 answers left." Plain sentence, no jargon. It states the one fact that matters before the client types anything.
2Badges are positional, not arithmetic. "Answer 5 of 10" reads like a page number and implies the remainder. The old "1 USED · CONVO PACK · 5 LEFT" made the reader do bookkeeping.
3Switching is progressive disclosure. With one chat item there is no control at all — the bar is a status line. The ▾ and item count appear only when a second item exists.
4Radio = in use; icon = card-scoped. Chat items get a selector because they compete for the next answer. Deliverables and calls never compete, so they are just links to their card.
5Free is labelled, not silent. Coach generosity stays visible — it is the strongest trust signal in the thread.

Coach · nothing left to draw from

Why two buttons beat a toggle

1State is in the button, not in the coach's memory. A toggle has a hidden position you can misread; a labelled button tells you the consequence at the moment you press it.
2"Send answer · 1 of 6" names the cost in the client's units, so both sides read the same number.
3Disabled, not hidden, when nothing is left — the coach learns why, and the route to fix it ("Offer more help") sits right beneath.
4The invariant is now visible in the UI: there is no control anywhere on this screen that charges more than the client already bought.

C6 · order card in thread

Rules that make this safe

1Inputs are collected before payment, so a coach never sits blocked and a client never pays for something that cannot start.
2The card updates in place — one card per item for its whole life, not a new message per state change.
3Approval is what releases money, and the card says so plainly, with the auto-approve date visible so neither side is hostage.
4Request changes keeps money in escrow and returns the item to In progress; unlimited rounds are not allowed — two, then it escalates to help.

C8 · review the item

Repeat purchase is the metric

1The rebuy card appears in-thread at the exact moment of need, priced at the same rate — no trip back to the menu.
2Free messaging never stops. Running out ends the meter, not the relationship — this is what makes returning feel natural rather than transactional.
3Reviews are per item and prompted once, on completion. Outcome chips are the structured proof that powers discovery.
4First → second purchase is the number that decides whether this business exists. Every screen in this flow is instrumented for it.

C10 · coach went silent (SLA)

Failure paths, spelled out

1Repair before refund. Options are ordered by how often they are the right answer, not by severity.
2Escrow makes the promise concrete: "your $30 is still in escrow" is a fact about where the money physically is, not a customer-service posture.
3A silent coach is the platform's problem, not the client's. Every item carries an SLA clock from the coach's own stated turnaround; breach surfaces a one-tap refund and auto-refunds if ignored.
4Nothing dead-ends. Every failure has a visible next action and a deadline attached to it.

W2 · onboarding checklist

Supply-side design notes

1The audition is the quality bar. Every applicant answers the same prompt, giving directly comparable work samples — the first cohort's quality is the product's reputation.
2Payouts before listing, not after the first sale. A coach who cannot be paid must never be able to take money.
3Three explicit gates beat a vague "complete your profile" — the coach always knows what stands between them and earning.

W3 · the composer

Three questions, any business model

1What × how delivered × how priced generates every offering in the marketplace — including combinations we never anticipated.
2"Ask me — I quote" is the escape hatch. Anything a coach dreams up that the three dials cannot express becomes a quote, so no coach is ever blocked by our roadmap.
3"You keep $3.20" shows the fee at the moment of pricing, in the coach's favour-framing. No payout-day surprises.
4The turnaround promise is a commitment, not a badge: it sets the SLA clock that protects clients and it is measured against reality.
5Live preview is WYSIWYG — it doubles as quality control on the coach's own copywriting.

W7 · earnings

Design rules

1Every figure is post-fee. A coach should never see a number that later shrinks.
2Refund rate is displayed to the coach because it drives their ranking. Making the ranking input visible is what makes it fair.
3Repeat rate sits beside rating — it steers coaches toward relationships rather than volume.
4Each line traces to a fulfilment ("bio approved"), so escrow is explained by the receipt itself.

Two product decisions assumed here

Unused pack answers are refunded at the coach's à-la-carte rate, not the discounted pack rate — otherwise abandoning a pack early is cheaper per answer than buying singles, which quietly penalises coaches for offering bundles.

Free first answers may be reviewed, but those reviews carry near-zero weight and do not count toward the review total — otherwise a coach could farm reviews through free items at no cost, defeating the rule that every review costs real money to fake.
