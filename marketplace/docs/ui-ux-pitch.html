UI/UX Design Pitch · v2 comprehensive · working title: "Wing"

What it looks like when human help is the product

Every screen, both sides of the marketplace. One rule everywhere: plain dollars, charged per item at the moment of purchase — no credits, no wallet to pre-fund, nothing to decode. The interface's job is to make paying a person feel natural, transparent, and worth it.

01

Design stance — four principles

Principle 1

Human-warm, not AI-neon

Soft paper grounds, an editorial serif for moments of voice, real faces. Deliberately the visual opposite of the glowing-gradient AI tools — the design itself argues "a person is here."

Principle 2

Dollars, stated plainly

Every price is a dollar amount, charged at purchase like any normal checkout. No token currencies, no conversion math, no pre-funding. "$4 per reply" means exactly that, on a card statement they recognize.

Principle 3

The chat is the product

One thread holds everything — advice, purchases, deliverables, offers — as cards in the stream. If it happened, it happened in the conversation.

Principle 4

Consent anchors every charge

The client picks the item; the coach fulfills it. Terms sit pinned atop the thread, and a coach action can cost what was agreed — or less. Never more.

02

Visual language

Warm off-white ground, plum-black ink, one confident rose accent reserved for money and primary actions, and a sage-teal that exclusively means "free / good news." Serif for human voice moments, sans for UI, mono only for dollar amounts and status badges — anything money-related is recognizable by typeface alone.

C4 · Purchase sheet

Why they look this way

1The menu is the profile — offerings as tappable rows with plain dollar prices, scannable like a restaurant menu. "Something else? → ask" makes the custom-quote path a first-class item.
2Checkout is a native-feeling sheet, not a page: item, price, saved card / Apple Pay, one button. Tap-to-paid in under three seconds — the whole micro-consulting bet depends on this being impulse-frictionless.

4No wallet anywhere. Buying a pack just means this purchase includes 10 replies — a deal on an item, not a balance system to understand.

C5 · Thread (pack engagement)

Why it looks this way

1The engagement strip is the consent anchor. The client bought the pack; its terms sit pinned atop the thread. A per-reply item shows "$4/reply · $8 so far"; a timed session shows a live countdown; a deliverable shows its order status.
2FREE badge in sage: Maya's clarifying question doesn't consume anything — her generosity is legible, which is both trust and her marketing.
3The grouped answer: two bubbles, one rose spine, one consumption — "1 reply used · 6 left." A real answer spans messages but counts once.
4Upsells are offer cards, never charges. Maya's bigger idea arrives priced in dollars with Accept / Not now — accepting runs the same purchase sheet as any item.
5Screenshots are first-class — labeled, blurred in previews, auto-expiring per the privacy stance.

W2 · Fulfillment queue

Why they look this way

1Three questions, three cards: the whole abstract pricing engine compresses to a form a non-technical coach finishes in 30 seconds — with a live WYSIWYG preview of the exact menu row clients will see.

4Custom requests arrive with two honest buttons — quote a price or decline politely. No ghosting path.

G4 · Custom quote → plan

Why they look this way

1The timer is the meter for live work — pinned, mutual, unambiguous. Overtime requires both parties to tap extend, so a session can never quietly overrun into money.
2Booking happens as a card in the thread (slots, confirmation, reminders) — no separate scheduling app to context-switch into.

4Deliverables carry judgment, not just facts: "45 min max", "playful beats candlelit", a graceful-exit option. The formats are designed to show off the human expertise that justifies the price.

G6 · Changes / refund

Why they look this way

1Subscriptions swap the meter for an INCLUDED badge — same thread, same primitives, but the anxiety of per-message cost disappears. This is the natural graduation for a client who's found their coach.
2The coach initiates in subscription mode (weekly check-ins) — retention comes from the coach feeling like a presence in your dating life, not a vending machine.
3The help flow leads with repair, not refund: request-changes first, refund second, report third — matching both fairness and the actual frequency of outcomes.
4Escrow makes the promise concrete: "your $30 is still in escrow — nothing has been paid out." The refund isn't a courtesy we might extend; it's your money that hasn't left.

G8 · Coach application

Why they look this way

1The marketplace isn't one demographic. Filters and specialties flex to whatever supply shows up — strategy for women, 40+ restarts, queer dating, approach anxiety. Specialties are coach-authored, so the catalog's language evolves with its experts.
2Different experts, different natural price points — $5 replies next to $45 deliverables next to free intro calls. The offering model absorbs all of it without special cases.

14

System notes

  • Billing model, precisely: every item is a normal Stripe charge at purchase time — saved card or Apple/Google Pay, one tap after the first purchase. Per-reply items charge per fulfillment on the saved card (batched into a single daily charge if several land the same day, so statements stay clean and per-transaction fees stay sane). Packs are just items that include N units. A stored-balance wallet is a possible later optimization for heavy users — it is not the launch mental model.
  • Component language: five money primitives render identically everywhere — FREE pill (sage), consumption badge (rose, mono), offer/quote card (always Accept/Decline), order card (PAID → DELIVERED → APPROVED in place), engagement strip (pinned terms). Learn them once, understand every screen.
  • Dark mode ships day one — this app gets used at 11pm in bed; same rose/sage semantics on a plum-black ground.
  • Motion: one signature moment — the consumption badge ticks ("6 left") when an answer lands. Everything else instant and quiet; respects reduced-motion.
  • Accessibility: money semantics never rely on color alone (badges carry words), 4.5:1 minimum contrast, full keyboard/screen-reader coverage on web.
  • Web first, app-shaped: mobile-web MVP (no app-store review risk for a dating-adjacent category at launch); desktop gives coaches a two-pane queue → thread workspace.
What to prototype first

Two make-or-break moments, both testable before any backend exists: the purchase sheet (does tap-to-paid feel as light as an impulse buy?) and the thread (does being metered by a human feel fair and legible?). Build both as clickable prototypes and user-test the feeling of paying. Everything else on these pages is a known marketplace pattern.
